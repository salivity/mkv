MKV Interlaced vs Progressive Video Flags Explained

The Matroska (MKV) multimedia container format uses standardized Extensible Binary Meta Language (EBML) elements within its video track headers to identify whether a video stream is interlaced or progressive. Rather than forcing the media player to decode raw video bitstreams to determine the scan type, MKV exposes dedicated container-level flags that explicitly state the display mode, scan type, and field order for seamless rendering and deinterlacing.

The EBML Video Track Structure

In the Matroska specification, stream configuration is organized hierarchically. Video metadata resides within the Tracks master element, specifically nested under TrackEntry and its child Video element (EBML ID: 0xE0).

Within the Video element, MKV uses two primary parameters to differentiate progressive content from interlaced content: FlagInterlaced and FieldOrder.

1. The FlagInterlaced Element (EBML ID: 0x9A)

The FlagInterlaced element is an unsigned integer flag that provides a high-level indicator of the video's scanning method. It accepts three standardized values:

  • 0 (Undetermined): The container does not declare whether the video is interlaced or progressive. In this state, the demuxer or video decoder must rely on codec-level metadata (such as H.264 VUI parameters or MPEG-2 sequence headers) to detect the scan type.
  • 1 (Interlaced): The video frames consist of alternating fields (fields recorded at different points in time), instructing the playback software or hardware to apply deinterlacing algorithms.
  • 2 (Progressive): The video frames are full, progressive frames where all scan lines were captured simultaneously.

2. The FieldOrder Element (EBML ID: 0x9D)

When a video is marked as interlaced (FlagInterlaced = 1), playback engines need to know the temporal sequence of the fields to prevent visual jitter and combing artifacts. MKV uses the FieldOrder element to specify which field should be drawn or processed first:

  • 0 (Progressive): Used when all fields are displayed simultaneously.
  • 1 (Top Field First / TFF): The top field (odd-numbered lines) occurs first in time, followed by the bottom field. This is the common standard for 1080i broadcast video.
  • 2 (Undetermined): Interlaced content with an unspecified field order.
  • 6 (Bottom Field First / BFF): The bottom field (even-numbered lines) occurs first in time. This is commonly found in standard-definition DV and DVD formats.
  • 9 & 14 (Field Swapped Modes): Advanced modes used in specific post-production scenarios where spatial and temporal field arrangements differ.

Container Flags vs. Codec Bitstream Flags

MKV's container-level scan flags act as a direct communication channel to media splitters, renderers, and video processors. While the underlying codec bitstream (such as HEVC, AVC, or MPEG-2) usually contains its own internal picture structure headers, container-level flags allow players to configure deinterlacing filters, hardware display synchronization, and aspect ratio scaling before the video decoder initializes the full bitstream. In the event of a conflict, compliant players typically allow container flags to override undetermined bitstream metadata.
