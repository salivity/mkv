Remuxing MKV to TS for Broadcast Transmission

Remuxing MKV (Matroska) files into TS (MPEG Transport Stream) format is a critical workflow in digital broadcasting that changes a video's container format without re-encoding the underlying audio and video streams. This article explains how video converters perform this process, transforming modern, file-based MKV media into broadcast-ready, packetized transport streams designed for error-resilient television transmission and real-time streaming networks.

What is Remuxing?

Remuxing, short for multiplexing modification, extracts elementary streams (video, audio, and subtitles) from one container format and places them into another without altering the original codecs or applying lossy compression. Because the media data is simply copied rather than decoded and re-encoded, the process is instantaneous, uses minimal processing power, and preserves 100% of the original audiovisual quality.

The Container Shift: MKV vs. MPEG-TS

MKV is a highly flexible, open-standard container designed for local storage, media players, and high-efficiency indexing. However, it is not optimized for linear, continuous transmission.

MPEG-TS (Transport Stream), defined by the MPEG-2 systems standard, is specifically engineered for unreliable broadcast channels such as terrestrial (DVB-T/ATSC), satellite (DVB-S), cable (DVB-C), and IP multicast. It organizes data into fixed-size packets that can be decoded on the fly, even if a receiver tunes into the middle of a transmission or encounters network packet loss.

The Technical Steps of the Remuxing Process

When a converter converts MKV to TS, it executes four fundamental stages:

  1. Demultiplexing (Demuxing): The converter parses the MKV container headers, clusters, and cues. It separates the interlaced data tracks into raw Elementary Streams (ES)—typically H.264/AVC, H.265/HEVC for video, and AAC or AC-3 (Dolby Digital) for audio.

  2. Packetization into PES (Packetized Elementary Stream): The continuous elementary streams are segmented into variable-length PES packets. The converter adds PES headers containing vital synchronization data, including:

    • PTS (Presentation Time Stamp): Dictates when a frame or audio sample must be displayed.
    • DTS (Decode Time Stamp): Dictates when a frame must be decoded (critical for modern video formats with out-of-order B-frames).

  3. TS Packet Generation (188-Byte Packets): The PES packets are further chopped into standard, fixed-size 188-byte TS packets. Each packet receives a 4-byte header that includes:

    • PID (Packet Identifier): A unique tag that identifies which stream (e.g., video, primary audio, secondary audio) the packet belongs to.
    • Continuity Counter: A 4-bit sequence counter (0–15) used by broadcast receivers to detect dropped packets.
    • Payload Unit Start Indicator (PUSI): Signals the beginning of a new PES packet.

  4. Generating PSI/SI Tables (Metadata & Timing): Unlike MKV, which relies on a single header at the start of the file, a TS stream must allow broadcast tuners to instantly identify channels and streams at any moment. The converter injects Program Specific Information (PSI) tables at regular intervals:

    • PAT (Program Association Table): Always on PID 0, this points to the available programs and their respective PMT locations.
    • PMT (Program Map Table): Defines the PIDs assigned to the video, audio, and subtitle streams within a specific program.
    • PCR (Program Clock Reference): Precision timing markers inserted directly into TS packet adaptation fields to synchronize the broadcaster's clock with the receiver's hardware clock.

  5. Multiplexing and Output: The TS packets from all streams, alongside the PSI tables and PCR markers, are interleaved based on their time stamps. If required for fixed-bandwidth broadcast standards, the converter inserts null packets (PID 8191) to maintain a constant bitrate (CBR). The resulting output is a standardized .ts file ready for broadcast playout servers and transmission hardware.
