Can MKV Encapsulate VC-1 from HD DVD and Blu-ray?

The Matroska (MKV) container format fully supports encapsulating VC-1 video streams extracted directly from both HD DVD and Blu-ray sources. This article covers how MKV handles the VC-1 codec, the lossless remuxing process from container formats like M2TS and EVO, hardware decoding considerations, and the primary tools used to perform the encapsulation without re-encoding.

Full Support for the VC-1 Codec

Matroska is a flexible, format-agnostic multimedia container designed to hold nearly any modern or legacy video, audio, and subtitle codec. VC-1 (standardized as SMPTE 421M) is officially supported within the Matroska specifications. Both Blu-ray and HD DVD commonly used the VC-1 Advanced Profile (AP@L3) to deliver 1080p high-definition content. When stored in an MKV file, this video stream retains its original bitstream integrity, metadata, and quality.

Lossless Remuxing (Direct Stream Copy)

Encapsulating a VC-1 stream into an MKV container does not require re-encoding or transcoding. The process is known as remuxing (multiplexing), which simply takes the raw elementary video stream from its original container and places it into the MKV structure.

  • Blu-ray Sources: Blu-ray discs store VC-1 streams inside MPEG-2 Transport Stream (.m2ts) containers. Remuxing extracts the VC-1 elementary stream and packs it into MKV seamlessly.
  • HD DVD Sources: HD DVD discs store VC-1 inside Enhanced VOB (.evo) containers. Remuxing extracts the video packets directly from the EVO structure into MKV without any quality loss.

Because the process only rewrites the container headers and packet packaging, remuxing is exceptionally fast and preserves 100% of the original video data.

Recommended Tools for Encapsulation

Several software utilities can extract and encapsulate VC-1 streams into MKV:

  1. MakeMKV: Reads directly from decrypted Blu-ray and HD DVD disc structures or ISO files, packaging the VC-1 video, audio tracks, and subtitles directly into an MKV file in a single step.
  2. MKVToolNix (mkvmerge): The standard tool for Matroska creation. It accepts extracted VC-1 elementary streams (.vc1) as well as M2TS and EVO files directly to output an MKV container.
  3. FFmpeg: A command-line utility capable of stream copying VC-1 from source files into MKV using the command -c:v copy.

Playback and Hardware Decoding Considerations

While MKV natively supports VC-1, modern playback compatibility depends on the media player and decoding hardware:

  • Software Decoding: Modern software players such as VLC, MPV, and MPC-HC (via LAV Filters) handle VC-1 in MKV effortlessly using CPU-based software decoding.
  • Hardware Acceleration (DXVA / NVDEC): Many newer GPUs and streaming devices (such as certain Android TV boxes or Apple TV units) have deprecated dedicated hardware decoding for VC-1 in favor of HEVC and AV1. In these scenarios, the playback device will fall back to software decoding, which requires a reasonably capable processor to maintain smooth 1080p playback.
