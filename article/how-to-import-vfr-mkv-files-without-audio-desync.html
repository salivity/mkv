How to Import VFR MKV Files Without Audio Desync

Importing Matroska (MKV) files recorded with Variable Frame Rates (VFR)—often generated by screen recorders like OBS, smartphones, and game capture software—frequently causes audio and video to drift out of sync in professional video editing software. Because non-linear editing systems (NLEs) rely on Constant Frame Rates (CFR) to accurately align timeline audio and video tracks, editors must standardize these files. This guide explains why desynchronization occurs and details the most reliable methods to convert and import VFR MKV files into constant frame rates without quality loss or timing errors.

Why VFR Causes Audio Desynchronization

Video editing software such as Adobe Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, and Final Cut Pro calculates time based on a fixed number of frames per second (FPS).

  • Variable Frame Rate (VFR): Dynamically adjusts the frame rate based on hardware load or motion intensity to save file size.
  • Constant Frame Rate (CFR): Maintains an identical duration for every single frame throughout the entire clip.

When an NLE interprets a VFR file as CFR, it spaces out variable-duration frames across a rigid timeline grid. While the audio plays back at real-time speed, the video stretches or compresses, leading to progressive audio drift over the duration of the clip.

Method 1: Transcode to CFR Using HandBrake (GUI)

The most common approach to fixing VFR MKV files without manual timeline adjustments is converting the file to CFR using HandBrake.

  1. Download and open HandBrake.
  2. Drag and drop your MKV file into the interface.
  3. Under the Summary tab, choose your preferred format (MP4 or MKV).
  4. Navigate to the Video tab:
    • Select your desired Framerate (FPS) (e.g., 60 or Match Source).
    • Check the radio button for Constant Framerate (CFR).
    • Set the Encoder Preset to Faster or Fast, and choose a Constant Quality (RF) value between 18 and 22 for visually lossless output.
  5. In the Audio tab, set the codec to Auto Passthru or AAC to preserve source audio.
  6. Click Start Encode.

Once complete, import the resulting CFR file directly into your NLE timeline.

Method 2: Convert to CFR Using FFmpeg (Command Line)

For batch processing or automated workflows, FFmpeg can convert VFR MKV files into production-ready CFR formats like ProRes or standard CFR H.264.

Option A: Standard H.264 CFR Conversion

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -vsync cfr -r 60 -c:v libx264 -crf 18 -preset fast -c:a copy output.mp4
  • -vsync cfr: Forces the video output into a constant frame rate.
  • -r 60: Sets the target frame rate to 60 FPS (adjust to match your target).
  • -c:a copy: Copies the audio stream directly to prevent generational loss.

Option B: High-Performance ProRes Conversion (Recommended for DaVinci Resolve & Premiere)

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -vsync cfr -r 60 -c:v prores_ks -profile:v 3 -c:a pcm_s16le output.mov

This produces an editing-optimized Apple ProRes file that eliminates CPU decoding bottlenecks while maintaining perfect synchronization.

Method 3: Remuxing vs. Transcoding

Many creators use tools like OBS to "Remux" MKV to MP4.

  • Remuxing merely changes the file container from .mkv to .mp4 without re-encoding the video. If the source MKV is VFR, the remuxed MP4 will remain VFR and will still desync in most NLEs.
  • Transcoding decodes the video frames and re-encodes them to fixed time intervals, which is the necessary step to eliminate VFR drift.

If your MKV is already recorded in CFR, OBS remuxing (File > Remux Recordings) is sufficient. If the recording has dropped frames or variable capture rates, you must transcode using HandBrake or FFmpeg.

How to Prevent VFR at the Source

To avoid conversion entirely, adjust your recording software to ensure native CFR output:

  • In OBS Studio: Use the Advanced Output Mode, set the rate control to CBR (Constant Bitrate) or CQP, and avoid overloading the GPU/CPU to prevent dropped frames from forcing variable timing.
  • In Smartphone Capture: Use dedicated camera applications (such as Blackmagic Camera or Filmic Pro) that enforce locked frame rates rather than native camera apps that default to VFR.
