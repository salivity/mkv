What Causes Audio Pops When Switching MKV Tracks

When switching between audio tracks in a Matroska (MKV) container during media playback, listeners frequently hear a sharp "pop," "click," or crackle. This article explores the root causes of these audio artifacts, which stem from instantaneous waveform discontinuities, buffer clearing, decoder reinitialization, sample rate mismatches, and the lack of transition smoothing in media playback software.

Waveform Discontinuity and Non-Zero Crossings

The primary cause of an audio pop is an abrupt jump in the audio signal's amplitude. Sound is represented digitally as a continuous alternating waveform. If an audio track is cut off at a point where the wave is at a high or low peak rather than at the zero-voltage line (known as a zero-crossing), the speaker cone is forced to instantaneously reset or jump to a different position. This rapid mechanical movement creates a transient voltage spike, which is heard as an audible click or pop.

Audio Buffer Flushing

Media players maintain a playback buffer containing several milliseconds to seconds of pre-decoded audio data. When an audio track switch is triggered, the player immediately purges the active buffer to prevent old audio from playing over the newly selected stream. If the player does not fade the outgoing volume down before dumping the buffer, the sudden cessation of the audio stream creates a digital hard-stop, resulting in a pop.

Codec and Hardware Reinitialization

MKV containers often store multiple audio tracks encoded in different formats, bitrates, or channel layouts (such as switching from a 2.0 AAC track to a 5.1 TrueHD or DTS track). When switching tracks, the media player must tear down the existing audio decoder and initialize a new one. This process can interrupt the digital-to-analog converter (DAC) or audio receiver (AVR), causing the hardware to momentarily lose clock sync and output a burst of static or a popping noise as it locks onto the new format.

Sample Rate and Clock Mismatches

If the alternate audio track uses a different sample rate (for instance, switching from a 44.1 kHz track to a 48 kHz track), the system audio engine must change its operating frequency. During this resynchronization, the hardware clock adjusts, causing a brief drop in signal continuity that presents itself as an audible glitch.

Lack of Crossfading or Volume Ramping

Modern, high-end audio engines mitigate popping by applying a micro-fade (a 5 to 20-millisecond fade-out on the old track and fade-in on the new track). However, many media players and video renderers switch streams via a hard cut to minimize switching latency, omitting the crossfade algorithm entirely and allowing the transient noise to pass directly to the output device.
