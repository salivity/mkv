Why Smart TVs Fail to Play DTS Audio in MKV Files

Many users experience silence or an "Audio Format Not Supported" error when playing MKV video files on modern smart TVs. This issue typically occurs because the television lacks the dedicated software license or hardware decoder required to process Digital Theater Systems (DTS) audio, despite supporting the MKV file format itself. Understanding the distinction between multimedia containers and audio codecs, alongside changing manufacturer licensing policies, explains why this playback failure happens and how it can be resolved.

Licensing and Royalty Cost Cutting

The primary reason smart TVs fail to decode DTS tracks is licensing. Including built-in DTS decoders requires TV manufacturers to pay patent licensing fees for every unit sold.

Over recent years, major manufacturers such as Samsung (since 2018) and LG (between 2020 and 2022, though partially reintroduced in select newer models) systematically phased out native DTS decoding support from their operating systems (Tizen, webOS). Because most streaming platforms (Netflix, Disney+, Amazon Prime Video) utilize Dolby Digital Plus (E-AC-3) rather than DTS, manufacturers chose to eliminate DTS licenses to lower production costs without affecting mainstream streaming performance.

Containers vs. Audio Codecs

A common source of confusion is that a TV might successfully play one MKV file while failing to play another.

  • The Container (MKV): Matroska Video (.mkv) is an envelope or container format that packages video, audio, and subtitle streams together. Most modern smart TVs natively support the MKV container.
  • The Codec (DTS): The actual audio stream inside that container is encoded using specific compression technology. If an MKV file contains a DTS, DTS-HD MA, or DTS:X audio stream, a TV without a DTS decoder will play the video smoothly while producing no sound or throwing an error.

Passthrough Limitations

Some smart TVs not only refuse to decode DTS internally for their built-in speakers but also block the raw DTS bitstream from passing through HDMI (eARC/ARC) or Optical (TOSLINK) connections to external soundbars and AV receivers. If the TV's internal media player cannot recognize the audio codec, it frequently drops the audio track entirely rather than transmitting it downstream to a DTS-capable sound system.

How to Fix DTS Audio Issues on Smart TVs

Users encountering unsupported DTS audio in MKV files can resolve the issue using several straightforward methods:

  1. Audio Transcoding: Convert the DTS audio track inside the MKV container to a universally supported format like AC3 (Dolby Digital), AAC, or PCM. Tools such as HandBrake, FFmpeg, or dedicated audio converters can transcode the audio track without re-encoding or degrading the original video stream.
  2. Dedicated Media Players: Use third-party media streamers (such as an Apple TV 4K, NVIDIA Shield TV, or Amazon Fire TV Stick 4K) running media apps like Plex, Jellyfin, Kodi, or VLC. These devices decode the DTS audio internally or handle passthrough directly to compatible receivers.
  3. Local Network Streaming with Real-Time Transcoding: Setting up a local Plex or Emby server automatically detects that the client TV cannot decode DTS and converts the audio to a compatible format on the fly during playback.
