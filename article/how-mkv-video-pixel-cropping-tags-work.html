How MKV Video Pixel Cropping Tags Work

The Matroska (MKV) multimedia container manages non-standard video dimensions by embedding dedicated pixel cropping tags directly into the track headers of the Extensible Binary Meta Language (EBML) structure. Instead of permanently altering the underlying encoded video stream or requiring lossy re-encoding to remove padding, MKV uses container-level metadata to instruct media players to trim specified edge pixels during playback, ensuring proper aspect ratios and display resolutions while preserving codec efficiency.

The MKV EBML Header Structure

In the Matroska specification, cropping values are stored as unsigned integer elements located within the video track metadata hierarchy: Segment > Tracks > TrackEntry > Video.

There are four distinct EBML elements dedicated to pixel cropping:

  • PixelCropTop (EBML ID: 0x54BB): The number of video pixels to remove from the top of the decoded image.
  • PixelCropBottom (EBML ID: 0x54AA): The number of video pixels to remove from the bottom of the decoded image.
  • PixelCropLeft (EBML ID: 0x54CC): The number of video pixels to remove from the left side of the decoded image.
  • PixelCropRight (EBML ID: 0x54DD): The number of video pixels to remove from the right side of the decoded image.

If any of these elements are omitted from the file, their values default to zero.

Why Pixel Cropping Is Necessary

Video codecs such as H.264 (AVC), H.265 (HEVC), and AV1 process frames in structured blocks called macroblocks or Coding Tree Units (CTUs). These processing blocks generally require video width and height to be divisible by specific values, commonly 8, 16, 32, or 64 pixels (referred to as mod-8, mod-16, etc.).

When a video has an arbitrary or non-standard resolution—such as an ultra-wide theatrical aspect ratio like 1920x804 (which is not divisible by 16)—encoders often pad the video to a compliant dimension like 1920x816. The MKV container resolves this misalignment by storing the full 1920x816 encoded stream while setting PixelCropTop to 6 and PixelCropBottom to 6. This satisfies both the compression block constraints of the codec and the intended visual framing of the content.

Interaction with Display Dimensions and Playback

During playback, the rendering pipeline executes the cropping logic in a specific sequence:

  1. Decoding: The media decoder processes the full, padded frame stored in the bitstream (defined by PixelWidth and PixelHeight).
  2. Cropping: The demuxer or video filter reads the PixelCrop tags and trims the specified number of pixels from the decoded raw frame buffer, yielding the clean resolution: \[\text{Clean Width} = \text{PixelWidth} - (\text{PixelCropLeft} + \text{PixelCropRight})\] \[\text{Clean Height} = \text{PixelHeight} - (\text{PixelCropTop} + \text{PixelCropBottom})\]
  3. Scaling: If non-square pixels or custom display aspect ratios are used, the player applies the DisplayWidth and DisplayHeight tags relative to the cropped area rather than the uncropped raw buffer.

This container-level mechanism ensures lossless handling of non-standard resolutions across compliant decoders and rendering engines without modifying the underlying compressed video elementary stream.
