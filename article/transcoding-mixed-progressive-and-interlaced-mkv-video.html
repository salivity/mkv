Transcoding Mixed Progressive and Interlaced MKV Video

Processing MKV files containing mixed progressive and interlaced frames requires a video transcoder to dynamically detect field structures, reconstruct native film rates, and deinterlace video sections on a frame-by-frame basis. This article explains the technical pipeline transcoders use to analyze stream metadata, apply inverse telecine, adaptively deinterlace combing artifacts, and output a standardized progressive stream without degrading native progressive segments.

Frame-Level Analysis and Detection

Mixed-format MKV files typically originate from broadcast television, DVD rips, or hybrid master files where native 24p film, 3:2 pulldown telecine, and true 60i/50i video are spliced together. Because container-level metadata can only set a single default scanning mode, modern transcoders rely on dynamic frame-level analysis.

Transcoders read the field flags embedded in the elementary video bitstream (such as H.264 or MPEG-2 stream headers) to see if a frame is flagged as progressive or interlaced. However, because metadata flags are frequently inaccurate, modern software employs spatial-comb detection algorithms. These algorithms evaluate pixel differences between adjacent scan lines to detect comb artifacts in real time before deciding how to process each individual frame.

Field Matching and Inverse Telecine (IVTC)

When handling content originating from film that was converted for broadcast, transcoders use field matching rather than standard deinterlacing.

  1. Field Reconstruction: The transcoder pairs complementary top and bottom fields across adjacent frames to recreate the original progressive 24 fps film frame.
  2. Decimation: Once the original progressive frames are reconstructed, the transcoder identifies and discards the redundant duplicate fields created during the 3:2 pulldown process.

This process preserves the original image resolution and avoids the vertical resolution loss inherent to standard deinterlacing filters.

Adaptive Deinterlacing

For true interlaced material—such as live sporting events, video cameras, or scrolling text overlays—fields cannot simply be woven back together. For these sections, transcoders switch to adaptive deinterlacing:

  • Spatial-Temporal Interpolation: Filters (such as YADIF, BWDIF, or neural-network-based scalers) interpolate missing lines in moving areas while preserving full resolution in static areas.
  • Conditional Filtering: The transcoder applies deinterlacing solely to frames exhibiting interlacing artifacts. Progressive frames pass through the filter untouched, preventing the blurring or softness that occurs when a deinterlacing filter is universally applied across an entire file.

Frame Rate and Timecode Normalization

Interlaced video often converts to double the frame rate during deinterlacing (e.g., 60i becomes 60p to preserve motion smoothness), while telecined film returns to 24p. Transcoders manage this difference in one of two ways:

  • Variable Frame Rate (VFR): The transcoder writes precise timecodes for each frame into the output container, allowing the film sections to play at 23.976 fps and the deinterlaced video sections to play at 59.94 fps.
  • Constant Frame Rate (CFR): If target device compatibility requires a fixed frame rate, the transcoder normalizes the stream (typically to 59.94p or 60p) by duplicating complete progressive frames during film sections while maintaining full temporal fluidity during native video sections. Audio tracks are continuously referenced against video presentation timestamps (PTS) to prevent drift throughout the conversion process.
