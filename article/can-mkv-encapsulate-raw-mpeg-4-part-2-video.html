Can MKV Encapsulate Raw MPEG-4 Part 2 Video?

This article examines whether the Matroska (MKV) multimedia container format can encapsulate raw MPEG-4 Part 2 Visual video bitstreams. It covers the technical capability of Matroska to store this video format, explains the specific container mechanisms used (such as native framing and compatibility modes), and outlines how elementary streams are packetized into the MKV structure.

Yes, a Matroska (MKV) container can fully encapsulate MPEG-4 Part 2 Visual video bitstreams. MPEG-4 Part 2—commonly associated with legacy codecs such as Xvid, DivX, and standard FFmpeg MPEG-4—is natively supported in the official Matroska specification.

Matroska Storage Methods for MPEG-4 Part 2

Matroska handles MPEG-4 Part 2 bitstreams through two primary encapsulation methods:

  1. Native MPEG-4 Mapping: Under native mapping, Matroska defines specific Codec IDs based on the stream's profile:

    • V_MPEG4/ISO/SP (Simple Profile)
    • V_MPEG4/ISO/ASP (Advanced Simple Profile)
    • V_MPEG4/ISO/AP (Advanced Profile)

    In this mode, global configuration headers—such as the Video Object Sequence (VOS) and Video Object Layer (VOL) data—are extracted from the stream and placed into the Matroska CodecPrivate element. The individual Video Object Planes (VOPs), representing discrete video frames, are stored sequentially within standard Matroska Block or SimpleBlock structures.

  2. VFW Compatibility Mode (V_MS/VFW/FOURCC): To maintain broad compatibility with older AVI-era software, MPEG-4 Part 2 streams can also be stored using Video for Windows (VFW) compatibility. In this format, a standard BITMAPINFOHEADER containing the FourCC (such as XVID, DIVX, or FMP4) is placed in the CodecPrivate block, and the frames are stored in standard frame packets.

Encapsulating Raw Elementary Streams

A raw MPEG-4 Part 2 elementary stream (an .m4v or .raw file) is a continuous byte stream containing start codes, sequence headers, and frame data. While MKV cannot store a raw byte stream as an unparsed, monolithic block, muxing tools (such as FFmpeg or MKVToolNix) easily parse the raw bitstream and package it into the container.

During the muxing process:

  • The continuous stream is parsed to identify start codes (0x000001XX).
  • Header configurations are separated into container-level track metadata.
  • Individual frames are time-stamped with explicit presentation timestamps (PTS) and decode timestamps (DTS), eliminating the need for the "packed bitstream" workarounds traditionally required by older containers like AVI.
