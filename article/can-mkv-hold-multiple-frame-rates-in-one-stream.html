Can MKV Hold Multiple Frame Rates in One Stream?

The Matroska (MKV) container natively supports multiple distinct frame rates within a single video stream through a technology known as Variable Frame Rate (VFR). Unlike traditional container formats that enforce a single Constant Frame Rate (CFR), MKV relies on independent timecodes for each frame. This article explains how MKV handles dynamic frame rates within one stream, the mechanisms that make it possible, practical applications, and considerations for playback and editing.

How MKV Implements Variable Frame Rates

MKV does not require a fixed frame rate to be defined at the container header level. Instead, the format structures video data using timestamped packets:

  • Presentation Timestamps (PTS): Every individual video frame (stored in Matroska Block or SimpleBlock elements) has its own distinct timestamp relative to the track's timecode scale.
  • Independent Frame Durations: Because each frame specifies precisely when it should be displayed, the duration between consecutive frames can vary dynamically throughout the video.
  • Timecode Formats: When encoding or muxing, Matroska commonly utilizes timecode files (such as Timecode Format v1 or v2). Format v1 specifies frame rate ranges for particular frame numbers, while Format v2 defines the exact millisecond timestamp for every single frame.

Common Use Cases for Multi-Framerate Streams

Embedding multiple frame rates within a single video stream serves several practical purposes:

  1. Mixed Content in Animation and Film: Media often combines film scenes (typically 23.976 fps or 24 fps) with native video effects, scrolling credits, or CGI rendered at 29.97 fps or 59.94 fps. MKV preserves the native frame rate of each segment without requiring frame duplication or telecine artifacts.
  2. Inverse Telecine (IVTC) Corrections: Restoring telecined broadcast content often yields variable cadences. MKV allows true 24 fps progressive frames to coexist with 30 fps interlaced or progressive sequences.
  3. Screen Recording and Live Captures: Screen recording software dynamically drops frame rates during static scenes (reducing the rate down to 1–5 fps to save bandwidth) and increases them during fast motion (up to 60 fps or 120 fps).
  4. Efficiency and File Size Reduction: By lowering the frame rate during low-motion scenes or static slides, the encoder avoids processing redundant frames, significantly lowering overall file size.

Codec and Container Compatibility

MKV acts as the envelope, but the underlying video codec must also allow dynamic timing:

  • Modern video codecs such as H.264 (AVC), H.265 (HEVC), VP9, and AV1 fully support variable timing information alongside MKV.
  • Legacy codecs designed strictly around constant rates (such as raw MPEG-2 elementary streams) can still be packaged with variable timestamps in MKV, though encoder and decoder support may vary.

Playback and Editing Considerations

  • Media Players: Modern software players like VLC, MPV, and MPC-HC read Matroska frame timestamps accurately, adjusting their display refresh and render cycles dynamically without stutter.
  • Video Editing Software (NLEs): Professional editing suites (such as Adobe Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, or Final Cut Pro) are designed around fixed timeline frame rates. Importing a multi-framerate MKV can lead to audio/video desynchronization or dropped frames, often requiring conversion to a constant frame rate before editing.
